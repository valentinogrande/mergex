Mergex Under Load

Every thread writes to its own node. The aggregate is assembled only when someone asks for it. Here is what that costs, and what it buys, measured against seven alternatives — including the one it loses to.

The shape

What it actually does

A Mergex<T> is a handle to a node in a tree. copy() registers a son and hands back its handle; move that into a thread and it writes only to its own cell. Nothing is shared on the write path.

A write also raises a dirty flag, then walks up flagging each ancestor's subtree_dirty — stopping at the first one already raised, so a burst of writes costs one walk, not one per write.

When someone calls get(), the root folds every dirty descendant into itself with the merge function the developer supplied at construction, deepest first. If nothing is dirty, that flag turns the whole sweep into a single atomic load.

write_throughput

Writes are the whole point

N threads, 20,000 writes each, then one read of the aggregate. Higher is better. The machine has twelve hardware threads, so the sweep runs past it into oversubscription. The shared-cell designs all peak at one thread and fall from there; the per-writer designs climb.

The split is clean and it is not about lock quality. Every design that funnels writes through one cell gets slower as threads are added — Mutex 140.5 → 21.8, RwLock 132.9 → 15.6, even a lock-free AtomicI64 291.5 → 52.1, because fetch_add still serialises on one cache line. Mergex and cache-line-padded sharding both climb instead, because no two writers touch the same line.

Unpadded sharding is the trap. One Mutex<i64> per thread in a Vec looks like the same idea and collapses to 39.1 M/s — 27× slower than the padded version. Adjacent shards land on the same 64-byte line, so the threads fight over cache even though they never share a lock. Mergex pays #[repr(align(64))] on its node for exactly this reason: without it the write benchmark is bimodal, swinging 3× on allocator luck alone.

Past the core count nothing collapses — it flattens. Mutex sits at 18.4 from twelve threads onward, RwLock at about 11.5, left-right at 8. They were already fully serialised at eight, so adding threads changes nothing: the queue is the queue. There is no lock convoy to see.

mergex peaks at twelve, not eight — 671 to 795 — and is the only entrant still climbing at the machine's physical limit, while padded sharding had already topped out. It also degrades best when oversubscribed: at 32 threads each structure holds mergex 91%, sharded_padded 85%, plain_local 70% of its own peak. With no shared lock, a thread descheduled mid-write blocks nobody. And the margin over a shared Mutex grows with thread count: 32× at eight, 43× at twelve, 41× at thirty-two.

And the honest ceiling on the win. Cache-line-padded sharding still beats mergex outright — 1018 against 795 at their respective peaks, in half the memory. If a flat one-level fold is all you need, hand-roll it. Mergex earns its keep with the tree: arbitrary depth, coalescing writes, self-cleaning membership, and an O(1) answer to "has anything changed?"

Two things in that chart are artefacts, not properties. Every series dips at 16 threads and recovers at 24 — 16 does not divide 12 evenly while 24 is exactly two full passes, so that is the scheduler, not the structures. And sharded is alone in improving with more threads, 21.4 at two to 79.9 at sixteen; the likely reason is that oversubscription leaves fewer threads genuinely simultaneous on a shared cache line, easing its false sharing. That one is a hypothesis, not a measurement.

readers

Reads are where it loses

A single reader on a quiet tree is the flattering measurement, and it is the one that hid the problem for most of this investigation. Add readers, or add a writer beside them, and the picture inverts.

Concurrent readers, with a writer running alongside

million reads per second — higher is better

Table view

mergex goes 3.8 → 4.8 million reads per second and stays there; left-right goes 7.7 → 119.1. The reason is structural, not a missing optimisation: get() takes the root's lock, so readers queue behind each other and behind the writer. left-right keeps two copies and swaps them on publish, so its readers are wait-free and never touch what the writer is touching. They are built for opposite problems, and each loses badly on the other's ground — left-right manages 10.2 million writes per second at eight writers, the slowest entry in the whole comparison.

If your workload is read-heavy, left-right is the right structure and this one is not. That sentence is the most useful thing on this page.

These two are the flattering pair, and they are real as far as they go. get_threaded() costs 7.10 ns, indistinguishable from holding a Mutex<i64> yourself. The single-reader aggregate is 10.87 ns and flat from 8 producers to 512, because subtree_dirty answers "is there work?" in one atomic load — while hand-rolled sharding must sum every shard and climbs to 3.65 µs, 336× worse. But single reader is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The chart above is the one that matters for a real workload.

What it costs in RAM

Live heap bytes, counted by a GlobalAlloc wrapper rather than estimated from size_of. Measured at 8, 64 and 512 producers; the slope is the marginal cost of one more writer.

Bytes per producer

marginal, from the 8 → 512 slope

Table view

A mergex node is 240 bytes: an Arc header, a Mutex<T>, a Mutex<Vec<Arc<Node>>> for its sons, a handle count, two flags, Arcs to the merge function and the identity, and the vector of ancestor flags — then rounds up to a cache line. That is 30× a bare i64 and just under 2× a cache-line-padded shard. Crucially it is the cost of a live producer: a worker that finishes and drops its handle is folded and unlinked by the next merge, so this figure tracks the live count rather than everything the process has ever spawned. The shared-cell designs are flat at 32–48 bytes because they have nothing per producer; they pay for concurrency in contention instead of memory.

Plain per-thread locals win outright and that is the honest ceiling — though it is also the least reproducible entrant here, because twenty thousand additions take a few microseconds and scheduler skew dominates. What they do not give you is aggregation: you fold them yourself, you cannot ask for a partial result mid-run, and there is no tree. The interesting neighbour is sharded_padded, up and to the left of mergex: faster, half the memory, and the thing to reach for when one flat level is all you need.

Method

How to read these numbers

The semantics are not identical, and cannot be. Mergex, sharding and plain locals write to a slot the thread owns. Mutex, RwLock and AtomicI64 read-modify-write one shared cell, because that is the only way a single cell keeps a running aggregate. Comparing them is the point — the question is what a running total costs — but they are not the same operation.

Thread spawn is inside the timed region for every entrant in the write benchmark, at 20,000 operations per thread, so it is amortised to under a percent and it is the same tax for all of them.

Sample sizes are criterion defaults (100 samples) except the write benchmark, which uses 20 because each iteration spawns threads. Every figure quoted here is a mean with a 95% confidence interval inside ±1% unless stated.

Two benchmark groups were rewritten after an earlier version of this page quoted them. copy and merge_dirty used criterion's iter_batched, which counts input teardown inside the timed region, so their figures scaled with the cost of dropping the tree rather than the operation. They now defer teardown and start the clock by hand; the Instant pair that costs is measured separately and subtracted.

An earlier version of this page said reads were a strength. That conclusion came from a single reader on a quiet tree, which is the best case and not a realistic one. Measuring N concurrent readers, with and without a writer alongside, reversed it. The reader-scaling chart is the correction.

Measured on a single machine, one run per configuration. Reproduce with cargo bench and cargo run --release --example memory.
